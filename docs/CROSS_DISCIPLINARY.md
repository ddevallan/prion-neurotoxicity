# Cross-Disciplinary Findings

Evidence from fields outside prion biology that informs the v5 model of prion neurotoxicity. Each finding was published under a different label — none of the original authors framed their work in the context of prion disease unless noted.

---

## 1. Antimicrobial Peptides (AMPs)

### Sub-lytic modulation of neuronal ion channels

- **Melittin potentiates AMPA receptors at sub-lytic concentrations (50 ng/mL)** with receptor-subtype selectivity — ruling out crude membrane disruption at that dose. Higher concentrations enhanced release of excitatory amino acids.
  Bhatt DK et al. *Biochem Biophys Res Commun* 1992.

- **NMDA receptors are mechanosensitive to bilayer tension.** Membrane stretch and arachidonic acid (a PLA2 product, activated by melittin) potentiate NMDA currents by alleviating the Mg²⁺ block. Any peptide that inserts asymmetrically or causes local thinning can modulate NMDA gating without direct binding.
  Bhatt DK et al. *PNAS* 2007.

- **LL-37 modulates P2X7 channels at sub-lytic concentrations**, triggering Ca²⁺ influx in a structure-dependent manner.
  Elssner A et al. *J Biol Chem* 2004.

- **Cationic arginine-rich peptides (CARPs) at sub-lytic doses cause NMDA receptor endocytosis**, *reducing* Ca²⁺ influx — demonstrating bidirectional modulation depending on mechanism (membrane insertion vs endocytosis).
  Bhatt et al. 2015; see also *Front Neurol* 2020 (PMC7412265).

### Dose-dependent signal inversion

- **LL-37 shows clean dose-dependent signal inversion.** Antimicrobial at 0.02–16 µM; eukaryotic cytotoxicity at ≥25 µM. In the brain: protective under physiological conditions (inhibits Aβ fibrillization, inhibits pyroptosis); causes significant AD phenotypes when accumulated — elevated Aβ, neurofibrillary tangles, neuronal death, impaired synaptic plasticity in mouse and monkey models.
  Mol Psychiatry 2022; doi:10.1038/s41380-022-01790-6.

- **CARP neuroprotection is charge-dependent, not sequence-specific.** Poly-arginine R18 at 1–5 µM reduces neuronal death after NMDA exposure by electrostatic blockade of the NMDA channel via guanidinium groups and endocytic internalization of NR2B subunits. Any sufficiently cationic peptide does this.
  *Front Neurol* 2020; PMC7412265.

- **Hormesis in antimicrobial peptides is documented.** Nisin and pediocin show biphasic dose-response on microbial growth: low-dose stimulation, high-dose inhibition.
  *BMC Microbiol* 2010; PMC2936355.

### PrP fragments are literally AMPs

- **PrP N-terminal has direct antimicrobial activity.** Kills Gram-negative (E. coli, P. aeruginosa), Gram-positive (B. subtilis, S. aureus), and fungi (C. parapsilosis). Activity maps to the N-terminal and KKRPKP heparin-binding domain. Mechanism: membrane perturbation, carpet-like, without helix induction.
  Pasupuleti M, Mangé A et al. *PLoS One* 2009; PMC2752989.

- **"Prionins" — 1,179 antimicrobial peptides hidden in prion proteins.** Deep-learning scan (APEX 1.1) of 19.3 million fragments from 2,897 prion and prion-like proteins found 1,179 AMP candidates. Of 75 synthesized: 59 inhibited bacterial pathogens, 53 perturbed membranes. Many were unstructured in water but adopted ordered conformations in membrane-mimetic environments (inducible folding). Two candidates comparable to polymyxin B in mouse skin infection.
  De la Fuente Lab, Penn. *Nature Microbiology* 2026; doi:10.1038/s41564-026-02408-1.

### Polyanion neutralization of AMPs

- **Heparin dose-dependently neutralizes cationic AMPs by electrostatic binding**, preventing their membrane interaction. This is an endogenous regulatory mechanism — the body uses glycosaminoglycans to control AMP activity and prevent inflammatory tissue damage.
  PMC10995223; Hayashida 2025, PMC12806164; Kaneider 2007, PMC5900850.

- **Heparan sulfate binds PrP N-terminal at exactly the predicted sites.** Three independent HS binding sites: residues 23–52, 53–93, and 110–128. The extreme N-terminal KKRPKP (23–28) is the strongest binding site. Cu²⁺ enhances binding at the octarepeat region (53–93).
  *J Biol Chem* 2002; doi:10.1074/jbc.M110994200.

- **Reinterpretation of pentosan polysulfate (PPS):** PPS inhibits PrPSc in cells but *stimulates* conversion cell-free — a longstanding paradox. Under v5: in cells, PPS neutralizes the AMP (freed N-terminal tail); cell-free, there is no toxicity to neutralize and PPS's conformational effects dominate. PPS failed clinically due to delivery (intraventricular infusion), not mechanism.
  CureFFI analysis, 2012.

## 2. Liquid-Liquid Phase Separation (LLPS)

### LLPS → amyloid is a validated general mechanism

- Confirmed for **α-synuclein** (LLPS-induced fibrils show *higher toxicity*; FEBS J 2024), **tau** (LLPS accelerates aggregation; multiple reviews 2025), **Aβ** (ACS Chem Neurosci 2024), **FUS** and **TDP-43** (condensates mature into gels and amyloid; Aging Dis 2024, PMC11346406). Now textbook.
  Reviews: Alberti & Dormann, *Dev Cell* 2020; Shin & Brangwynne, *Science* 2017.

- **Same sequence regions drive both LLPS and amyloid formation** across all systems. For TDP-43, Phe-Gly motifs drive both (PMC8109789). For α-synuclein, N-terminal and NAC regions.

- **Two distinct thresholds**: Csat for LLPS onset, and a higher threshold for the liquid-to-solid transition.

### PrP-specific LLPS findings

- **PrP N-terminal polybasic motifs drive LLPS**, initiating conformational conversion — a pathway distinct from classic template-seeding.
  Yi Liang group, Wuhan. *JBC* 2021; PMC8289115.

- **Dog PrP residues Arg177 and Asp159 slow LLPS** and inhibit amyloid formation of human PrP — a structural explanation for canine prion resistance independent of the N-terminal toxicity model.
  *JBC* 2023; PMC10641668.

- **Post-LLPS conformational shift** can be modulated by polymeric antagonist (PSCMA) and mutations, positioning condensates as an intermediate between PrPC and PrPSc.
  *JACS* 2024.

- **GPI membrane anchor suppresses PrP phase separation** through topological confinement — restricted conformational degrees of freedom limit the intermolecular interactions underlying LLPS. Released from membranes, PrP spontaneously transitions to insoluble aggregates. The N-terminal domain is "required and sufficient" for LLPS independently of anchor status.
  Tatzelt Lab, Ruhr-Bochum. *PNAS* 2025; PMC11725851.

- **G127V promotes droplet fluidity** and negatively modulates LLPS, significantly inhibiting amyloid fibril formation — keeps condensates liquid rather than preventing condensation entirely.
  *J Neurochem* 2023; doi:10.1111/jnc.15586. *Commun Biol* 2020; PMC7391680.

- **Copper drives PrP phase separation** at physiological ratios (1:1 to 1:4 Cu²⁺:PrP). Copper alone keeps condensates liquid and stable (proposed physiological function: copper buffering at synapses). **Copper + H₂O₂ triggers the liquid-to-solid transition** → amyloid with dityrosine cross-links.
  Evans, Li et al. *Sci Adv* 2023; PMC10624353.

### Therapeutic relevance

- Small-molecule "c-mods" (condensate modifiers) that stabilize liquid condensates and prevent liquid-to-solid transition are an active drug-discovery area.
  Trends Pharmacol Sci 2026; FASEB J 2025.

- Myricetin slows tau LLPS and inhibits α-synuclein aggregation by delaying the transition.

- **No one has tested condensate-targeted compounds in prion models.**

## 3. Membrane Biology and Curvature

### AMPs and membrane curvature

- **AMPs preferentially bind and insert into curved membranes.** The mechanism is lipid packing defects — gaps in the headgroup layer exposing the hydrophobic core at regions of positive curvature. Binding increases monotonically with curvature. Smaller vesicles (higher curvature) lyse at lower peptide concentrations.
  Vanni et al. *JBC* 2008; *J Phys Chem Lett* 2024, PMC10788957; *ACS Chem Biol* 2010.

### Synaptic vulnerability

- **Dendritic spine necks are among the highest-curvature structures in neurons.** Spine neck radius: 50–200 nm; head: 250–500 nm. Spines are enriched in lipid rafts and cholesterol — precisely where GPI-anchored PrPC resides.
  *Front Physiol* 2021; *Front Neuroanat* 2014, PMC4126552.

- **α-Synuclein preferentially binds highly curved synaptic vesicle membranes** — the direct precedent for a curvature-sensing amyloidogenic protein at synapses.
  *J Neurochem* 2019.

- **Prediction for prion disease:** the freed PrP N-terminal AMP preferentially perturbs membranes at spine necks (highest curvature, most packing defects) → spine neck collapse before head loss → consistent with observed dendritic beading.

### Lipid raft heterogeneity

- **Cholesterol protects against AMP insertion in simple bilayers** by increasing membrane cohesion. **But cholesterol does NOT inhibit AMP disruption at raft boundaries** — lipid heterogeneity at raft edges creates insertion points.
  *Biophys J* 2019; PubMed 22885355.

- **Lipid raft composition varies by brain region and cell type.** A comprehensive human brain lipidome atlas (419 lipid species) shows 84% correlate with cell-type markers.
  *Nat Commun* 2024.

- **Sphingomyelin depletion → 4× increase in PrPSc formation** (inverse correlation). Different lipids produce different PrP conformations in vitro — the local lipid environment may steer which strain conformation forms.
  PMC9871914; PMC4474664.

- **The lipid raft is the intersection of all three processes**: (1) conversion platform (strain-dependent cofactor), (2) AMP insertion target, (3) regional variability. No one has integrated these three literatures.

## 4. Structural Analogies

### Auto-inhibition released by oligomerization

- **NINJ1 (Ninjurin-1)** — the closest structural parallel to PrP. At rest: auto-inhibited homodimer sequesters the hydrophilic membrane-rupturing face. On cell death signaling: dimer dissociates and re-oligomerizes into fence-like filaments that punch disks out of the plasma membrane. Release mechanism is change of oligomeric state, not phosphorylation or cleavage. Directly cytotoxic.
  *Nature* 2024.

- **MLKL** — 4-helix bundle auto-inhibited by pseudokinase domain. Tetramerization releases the bundle. Released 4HB alone causes membrane leakage; with support helix, it does not. Monobody stabilizing the auto-inhibited conformation blocks death. Amphipathic N-terminal helices drive necroptosis through an antimicrobial peptide-like mechanism.
  Murphy et al. *Immunity* 2013; Hildebrand et al. *PNAS* 2014; Samson et al. *Nat Commun* 2020.

- **Gasdermin D** — C-terminal domain sequesters the N-terminal pore-forming domain. Cleavage by inflammatory caspases releases the N-terminal, which oligomerizes into membrane pores. Structural basis of autoinhibition well-characterized.
  Liu et al. *Structure* 2018.

### General principle: amyloid fuzzy coat

- **Amyloid fibrils universally consist of an ordered core flanked by disordered regions (the "fuzzy coat").** These flanking IDRs mediate membrane binding/disruption and chaperone binding in toxic amyloid assemblies. The core locks into place, freeing the flanks for new interactions. This is the exact structural logic of the PrP v5 model generalized to all amyloids.
  *JBC* 2023; PMC7736610.

- **The structural logic**: the intramolecular contact surface (folded domain holding the disordered tail) overlaps with the intermolecular oligomerization interface. When the globular domain forms the fibril core, the surface that held the disordered region is physically occupied.

### Oligomerization motifs

- **N-WASP** — disordered VCA effector sequestered by intramolecular GBD contact. Cdc42 releases VCA, but oligomerization/clustering by adaptor proteins (Nck, TOCA1) is required for full Arp2/3 activation. Valence/clustering amplifies the signal.
  Banjade & Rosen, *eLife* 2014; 3:e04123.

- **CARD9/CARD11** — CARD domain auto-inhibited by coiled-coil domain. Interface disruption triggers polymerization into helical filaments.
  *Nat Commun* 2019.

## 5. Charge and Severity Gradient

### Net positive charge of amyloid flanking regions

| Protein | Flanking region | Net charge (pH 7.4) | Membrane mechanism | AMP activity |
|---------|----------------|---------------------|-------------------|-------------|
| **PrP** | N-terminal 23–93 | **+7 to +9** | Carpet/detergent (no helix) | **Yes** (Mangé 2009) |
| **α-Synuclein** | N-terminal 1–60 | **+4** | Amphipathic α-helix | **Yes** (Park 2016) |
| **Tau** | N-terminal + C-terminal | Net positive (polyampholyte) | Electrostatic to anionic lipids | Not demonstrated |
| **Aβ42** | Full peptide (no flanking) | **−3** (pI ~5.3) | Oligomeric pore formation | **No** (anionic) |

### Correlation with disease severity

The severity gradient is consistent with charge: prion diseases (highest charge, +7 to +9, GPI-concentrated) kill in months; synucleinopathies (+4, cytoplasmic) progress over years; tauopathies (polyampholyte) over decades; Aβ-driven AD (anionic, different mechanism) over decades.

**Caveat:** This mapping is confounded by replication rate (prions ~1000× faster than tau), cellular localization, and regional selectivity. Charge alone does not prove causation but is consistent and testable (synthetic peptides with +4, +6, +8 in dendritic spine assays).

### Cross-species conservation

N-terminal charge is **identical (+6.5)** across all tested mammals — susceptible (human, mouse, hamster, sheep, cow, elk, bank vole) and resistant (dog, rabbit, horse). Resistance comes from the globular and hydrophobic domains, not the N-terminal tail. This confirms v5: toxicity mechanism is generic (same tail = same AMP); specificity is in conversion.

## 6. Kinetic Comparison

### Replication rates

| System | Doubling time | Relative to prion |
|--------|--------------|-------------------|
| Prion (hamster Sc237) | ~2 days | 1× |
| Prion (mouse RML) | ~5 days | ~2.5× slower |
| Tau (P301S mouse) | ~14 days | ~7× slower |
| Tau (human brain) | ~5 years | **~900× slower** |
| α-Synuclein (in vitro) | — | **~1000× slower** |

Sources: Meisl et al. *Nat Struct Mol Biol* 2021; CureFFI 2013; Meisl 2025, *Alz & Dementia*.

### Qualitative difference

Meisl 2025 argues the "prion-like" label is misleading. Prions are **truly autocatalytic** (exponential self-replication via fragmentation-elongation). Tau and α-synuclein spreading is rate-limited by cell-autonomous amplification and clearance, not by prion-like spreading. The kinetic mechanism is different, not just slower. The ~1000-fold difference in replication rate, combined with true autocatalytic fragmentation, accounts for months vs decades without requiring different toxicity mechanisms.

## 7. Sporadic Disease and Endosomal Nucleation

### The endosome as conversion site

- **Multivesicular body (MVB)/late endosome is the major intracellular site of prion conversion.** pH 4.5–5.5 destabilizes PrP. On intraluminal vesicles, PrP ectodomain is exposed to the endosomal lumen — the GPI topological brake is released. Concentration is massively increased by geometric compression.
  *J Cell Sci* 2015; PMC4379730.

- **Blocking macropinocytosis prevents PrPC → PrPSc conversion** — conversion requires internalization.
  *PLoS One* 2009; PMC2671965.

### Three converging age-related vulnerabilities

1. **Endosomal transit** — constitutive low-level risk from normal PrP recycling.
2. **Copper/ROS dysregulation** — copper-PrP condensates become pathological when oxidative stress triggers the liquid-to-solid transition (Evans Sci Adv 2023).
3. **Lipid raft degradation** — aging reduces sphingolipid/cholesterol content that stabilizes GPI-anchored PrP on the surface.

The ~1/million/year sporadic CJD incidence reflects the probability that all three factors align in the same cell. No somatic PRNP mutations were found in 205 sCJD cases (2024; PMC11328154).

## 8. Moir-Tanzi Parallel (Aβ as AMP)

Moir and Tanzi showed Aβ is an ancient innate immune effector whose oligomerization mediates pathogen entrapment. A 2018 review explicitly links PrP and Aβ: "PRNP and Aβ share membrane-penetrating, nucleic acid binding, and antiviral properties with classical antimicrobial peptides such as LL-37."

The "friendly fire" framing — an endogenous antimicrobial defense that becomes pathological when chronically activated — was first proposed for Aβ and applies directly to the PrP N-terminal under v5.

Sources: Moir & Tanzi, *Alz & Dementia* 2018; *Sci Transl Med* 2016; PubMed 30480243.
